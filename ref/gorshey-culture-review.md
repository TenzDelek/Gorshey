**Review of the gorshey diorama brief: five risky claims checked, plus other problems**

**1. Circle direction: overconfident, and the evidence covers a different dance**
- Every clockwise source I could check is about guozhuang (Chinese 锅庄/果卓; Tibetan sgor bro, "gordro"). That is the Kham, Yunnan and Qinghai circle dance, not gorshey (sgor gzhas).
  - ihchina 12933 (Chamdo) says "按顺时针方向边歌边舞" (singing and dancing clockwise).
  - In ihchina 10774, the clockwise line ("向顺时针方向移动") is about 果卓 (guozhuang). Its Dranang gorshey entry gives no direction.
  - Bing Liu's paper is about Zhuo/guozhuang.
- Both statements I found that are specifically about gorshey say counterclockwise:
  - Wu Yunming on Lhoka gorshey: "沿逆时针方向行走到歌唱结束" (they walk counterclockwise until the singing ends).
  - Houseal, Buddhistdoor: dancers "move in a counterclockwise circle". I saw this only in a search snippet, because the page returned 403.
- The only gorshey evidence for clockwise is the researcher's own reading of two exile videos. I could not watch them, so it is unverified.
- The kora argument is an inference. A dance ring around a fire or a speaker is not circling a sacred object.
- The geometry in the brief is internally correct: clockwise from above means dancers facing the centre drift to their own left, with the centre on the right shoulder when they face the way they travel.
- **Corrected statement:** "Kham, Yunnan and Qinghai gordro/guozhuang is documented as clockwise. For Ü-Tsang and exile gorshey, written sources say counterclockwise (Lhoka; Houseal), while one unverified video reading says clockwise. Treat gorshey direction as unresolved. Confirm with a Tibetan dancer, or check three or more exile videos and note where the camera is."
- Delete the avoid-list rule "Do not default to counterclockwise". It contradicts the sources that are actually about gorshey.

**2. Holding hands vs linking arms: "linked arms" is not supported**
- The sources say:
  - 拉手成圈 (holding hands to form a ring) in Chamdo.
  - 手拉圆圈 (hands joined in a ring) in Dranang gorshey.
  - 连手舞蹈 (linked-hands dance) for gorshey, per Wu.
  - 互拉手或搭肩 (holding hands or hands on shoulders): Wu says this about farming-area *guozhuang*, not gorshey.
  - 体前或体后交叉拉手 (hands crossed in front of or behind the body) in Lhatse. That entry is about tö-shey (stod gzhas), which is a different dance accompanied by the six-string lute (六弦琴), not gorshey.
- "lian bi ta ge" (连臂踏歌) is a Tang-dynasty Chinese literary phrase, not an ethnographic description. Drop it.
- **Corrected statement:** "In the traditional form, dancers hold hands in one ring, men on one side and women on the other, so men and women join hands where the two halves meet. Some guozhuang uses hands on shoulders. Tö-shey uses a crossed hand-hold. No source shows elbow-linking." The claim that exile dancers usually don't hold hands is plausible, but it comes only from the researcher's video viewing.

**3. Pangden as a married women's garment: stated too firmly**
- The CTA page (tibet.net) says there is no historical documentation of where the custom comes from. It gives two explanations: it marks marriage, or it marks reaching adulthood at a certain age.
- It also says the pangden is worn "mainly by the women from U-Tsang", is "not prevalent in the Amdo and Kham regions", and that today "even unmarried girls wear it as part of costume".
- The construction details (three panels, offset stripes, brocade corners) are not in the cited source. They match common examples but are unverified.
- **Corrected statement:** "A striped apron mainly from Ü-Tsang, widely associated with married or adult women (its origin is undocumented). Today young unmarried women wear it too. Not worn by men, and not traditional in Kham or Amdo."

**4. Prayer flag colour order: mostly right, but one rule is invented**
- Blue, white, red, green, yellow is the order most sources give (the Rubin Museum DIY guide, Karuna).
- Some sources disagree. Salisbury University gives blue, white, red, yellow, green. Sherpa Adventure Gear gives the reverse. Samye Institute says the colours are arranged by element rather than in a fixed line.
- Wikipedia does not give a left-to-right order. It describes vertical flags (darchog) as "usually large single rectangles attached to poles along their vertical edge". The brief's "yellow at the bottom" rule for vertical flags is not documented, so drop it.
- **Corrected statement:**
  - Horizontal strings repeat blue, white, red, green, yellow (sky, wind, fire, water, earth).
  - Vertical darchog are often a single large flag on a pole.
  - Flags never touch the ground, and old flags are burned.

**5. Step cycle: invented choreography with false precision, plus mechanical errors**
- Only these points are verified:
  - "3步一变、顿地为节" (the step changes every three steps; stamping marks the beat), Wu.
  - Heavy, full-sole jump landings in the fast section, Wu.
  - A slow sung walking opening, then a shout ("呀-呀!", or "休休休!" in Lhoka), then a fast section.
- Houseal's hop-step and eight-count rhythm could not be checked (403).
- The centimetre distances and the 32-count structure appear in no source.
- Much of the movement vocabulary comes from Chinese conservatory stage training: the five traits (颤开顺左绕: tremble, open, same-side, left, circle), the step-back/tread-forward step, and the "seven hand actions". That is the state stage aesthetic the brief's own avoid list warns against. "Phoenix wings" is also Chinese imagery.
- Mechanical bugs:
  - The stamps on counts 4, 8 and 12 must be weightless taps. Otherwise the next count, where the same foot steps again, is impossible.
  - In Phrase D, counts 29–31 "repeat" a four-count unit in three counts.
- **Corrected statement:** "Gorshey is a repertoire: each song has its own fixed steps. Model one real song from a Tibetan-made tutorial and credit it, or label the loop as a stylised approximation."

**Other corrections**
- **Naming:** "gorzhey" is not a real term. Use gorshey (sgor gzhas) for the Ü-Tsang farming-area form (Shigatse, Gyantse, Lhoka) and gordro (sgor bro, Chinese 锅庄) for Kham and Amdo. I'm fairly but not fully confident in "gordro".
- **Bonfire:** a bonfire at the centre at night is closely tied to tourist bonfire-evening shows (篝火晚会). The documented centrepiece is a jug of chang and khata scarves (Chamdo). An exile Lhakar scene would have a speaker, daylight and no fire. If you keep the bonfire, label the scene a composite.
- **Arms:** ihchina says *women* in Chamdo free the right arm and drape the sleeve behind. Men freeing the right arm is common, but don't cite 12933 for it.
- **Unsourced claims to drop or mark as observation:** "sessions open with a butter lamp and prayer"; "80% of the time facing centre"; the 54–72 BPM slow tempo. The strong peaks at 108 and 215 suggest the real slow pulse may be about 108 with a half-time feel.
- **Source quality:** Tibet Universal Travel, Maigoo and tibetantrekking are travel or aggregator sites. Bing Liu is a low-tier conference paper. ihchina reflects PRC state framing. Use tibet.net and Lhakar groups for exile practice.
- **Swastika:** the yungdrung is a legitimate Tibetan symbol, especially in Bön. Avoiding it is only to prevent misreading by outside viewers; reword the bullet so it doesn't imply the symbol is offensive.
- **Political sensitivity:** if the scene is set inside Tibet, do not show Dalai Lama imagery or Lhakar captions.
- **Confirmed as correct:** left-over-right chuba wrap; the 2006 burning of fur pelts; clergy do not dance gorshey; Bön circling counterclockwise; Wednesday as the Dalai Lama's soul day (la-za).
